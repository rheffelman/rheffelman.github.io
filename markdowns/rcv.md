In 2019, Jared Golden was elected as the U.S. representative for Maine's 2nd congressional district. He was one of the first congress members to be elected via ranked-choice voting. Bruce Poliquin, Golden's Republican opponent who lost by a slim margin, filed a federal lawsuit saying that the voting system is unconstitutional. The ranked-choice ballot data for this election is public, so this article will go over some interesting things I fund in the data with some Tableau data visualizations, as well as my data-oriented take on if this election was unfair/unconstitutional. 

## The Results
The official results of the election elect the Democrat candidate Jared Golden because while Bruce Poliquin had the *plurality* of first place votes (the largest number of first place votes), the ranked-choice voting system doesn't care solely about plurality, and tries to take the whole rank distribution into account. The particular voting system used is known as [Instant Runoff Voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant-runoff_voting) if you want to learn more about *why* Jared Golden was elected over Bruce Poliquin, given this voting data.

So what's the big deal? Why is this controversial? Under a "traditional" voting system Poliquin would have won, and under a ranked-choice voting system Golden wins, and this election was a ranked-choice voting election, so Golden wins. Fair, right? Well, ranked-choice voting isn't exactly a voting system, it's more like a *kind* of voting system. Here you can see the tabulations of this election under 9 different ranked-choice voting systems, most of which are contenders for being the "best" voting system.

https://public.tableau.com/views/FinalProjectHeffelmanRyan/VotingSystemTabulations?:showVizHome=no&:embed=true

As you can see, under 4/9 of these voting systems, Bruce Poliquin wins. Under 4/9 of these, Jared Golden wins. And in the remaining voting system (Majority), no one wins. So ultimately, Jared Golden won the election because the particular ranked-choice voting system that was chosen happened to be one that elected him, as opposed to the numerous equally valid ranked-choice voting systems that did not, e.g. Borda count and . Was IRV chosen for the election because it's the "best" ranked-choice voting system? Well Arrow's impossibility theorem tells us there's no such thing as a "best" ranked-choice voting system. But is IRV the "best" system out of the ones that are known? No, IRV isn't demonstrably better than a system like a Borda Count (which elects Poliquin). IRV is better than a Borda Count in certain *ways*, for instance it satisfies the majority criterion, and then Borda Count is better than IRV in certain *ways*, for instance it satisfies monotonicity, but these *ways* are incomparable. Who's to say if monotonicity is a better quality of a voting system than meeting the majority criterion, it's intangible. It's like asking if "a" is better or worse than "1".
